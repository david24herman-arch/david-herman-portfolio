01 · About

Learning to communicate like an engineer.

Before taking ENG 2003, I expected the course to be mostly about grammar, report formatting, and presentations. Those things were part of it, but the course ended up being more about understanding why a message works for one audience and not another.

That changed how I think about engineering communication. Being technically correct is important, but it is also important to think about what the reader needs, what they already know, and what they are supposed to do with the information.

The pieces in this portfolio are arranged from my earliest and weakest work to the work I consider my strongest.

Looking at them together makes the development easier to see. I started by explaining ideas mostly in the order I found them. Later, I became more aware of audience, source quality, structure, visuals, and revision.

The goal of this portfolio is not to make every piece look perfect. I wanted the collection to show the process of getting better.

01

Economics Reflection

ENG 2001 · Starting point

I put this reflection first because it shows where my writing was near the beginning of the year. It was a short assignment about what stood out to me in an economics lecture, and I focused on monopsony because it was a concept I had not thought about before.

I tried to explain it through cause and effect instead of just repeating the definition from class. The language is straightforward, and the example of one powerful buyer controlling a market made the idea easier to understand.

At the same time, the reflection stays fairly close to the lecture and does not go very far into my own experience or connect the idea back to engineering. Looking at it now, I can see that I was explaining what I learned, but I was not really shaping the piece for a reader yet.

This is probably the weakest piece in the portfolio, but that is also why I wanted to include it. It gives the rest of the work a starting point.

What changed

I was mainly focused on explaining the idea itself. I had not yet started thinking as carefully about audience and purpose.
02

Professional Cover Letter

ENG 2003 · Learning to write for an audience

The cover letter changed the way I thought about audience. I could not just describe myself in general. I had to look at what the employer wanted and decide which parts of my experience actually belonged in the letter.

I used my work on an Arduino-based automated seat, my role in testing and debugging it, and my interest in restoring vintage hand planes because those examples showed patience and practical problem-solving.

This assignment was more uncomfortable than the economics reflection. I find it easier to explain how a system works than to explain why someone should hire me. I did not want to sound like I was exaggerating, but being too careful can also make a cover letter sound unsure.

Working through that balance helped me become more direct about what I contributed to a project.

What changed

I started choosing information based on the person reading it, rather than only based on what I wanted to say.
03

Annotated Bibliography

ENG 2003 · Becoming a more careful researcher

By the time I reached the annotated bibliography, the work became more technical. I chose small modular reactors because the topic connects energy demand, electrical grids, thermodynamics, and the move toward lower-carbon power.

At first, I mostly thought the job was to find five credible sources. The assignment made me realize that finding a source is only the beginning.

For every source, I had to separate what it said from how useful it actually was. A BBC article gave me an accessible overview, but it could not carry the technical part of the report. Government material helped with Canadian regulation and waste management, while journal articles were more useful for thermodynamic efficiency and microgrid integration.

The bibliography pushed me outside my comfort zone because I could not hide behind a summary. I had to make a judgement and support it.

Research process
Find → Evaluate → Compare → Use
What changed

I learned that a credible source is not automatically the right source. I had to explain what each source could and could not contribute.
04

Technical Report

ENG 2003 · My strongest work

The technical report is the last piece because it is where the earlier work comes together. I began with a topic I was genuinely interested in, but interest alone was not enough to make the report clear.

I had to move from the basic operation of a Small Modular Reactor into its advantages, limitations, and possible role beside renewable energy. That meant deciding what background the reader needed and what could be left out.

The first draft had a good overall structure, but peer feedback showed where it was still thin. I added a diagram explaining how an SMR generates electricity, comparison tables, and a Canadian example based on the Darlington project.

I also made the discussion of cost and uncertainty more careful. Those changes taught me that revision does not always mean starting over. Sometimes the better decision is to keep the parts that work and add detail exactly where the reader needs it.

This assignment pushed me the furthest outside my comfort zone because of its length and because I had to explain nuclear technology without assuming the reader already understood it.

It is my strongest piece, although I would not call it perfect. What makes it my best work is that it is organized, supported by research, and balanced. I did not present SMRs as the only answer. I argued that they may be one useful part of a larger energy system, which is closer to how real engineering decisions are made.

From research to technical argument
Energy demand → SMR technology → Grid integration → Balanced conclusion
What changed

This is where audience analysis, research, organization, visuals, peer feedback, and revision came together.
03 · Reflection

What I learned.

The biggest change was realizing that engineering communication is not just about getting the technical information right.

Part A — Overall Course Reflection

Before taking ENG 2003, I expected a course mostly about grammar, report formatting, and maybe presentations. Those things were part of it, but the course was more about understanding why a message works for one audience and not another.

That was useful because engineering communication is not only about whether the information is technically correct. It also matters what the reader needs, what they already know, and what they are expected to do with the information.

Three assignments made that clear to me. The cover letter forced me to choose which experiences mattered to a particular employer instead of writing a general description of myself. The annotated bibliography made me separate summarizing a source from evaluating it.

Finally, the technical report made me organize a large amount of information so a reader could move from the basic operation of an SMR to its benefits, limitations, and possible role in the electrical grid.

The peer-review stage was probably the most useful part because another reader noticed where I needed a diagram, stronger evidence, or more context even when the meaning seemed obvious to me.

The communication axioms helped me understand this. A message has both a content and a relationship side, so credibility and tone affect how the technical content is received. Communication is also unavoidable: formatting, missing information, and even a crowded page communicate something.

I found audience analysis and visual communication the most interesting because they changed the way I looked at documents I had previously thought of as mainly written text.

I tried to apply the 7 C's by making the report clear, concrete, correct, coherent, complete, concise, and courteous. The difficult part was balance. Being concise did not mean removing the limitations of SMRs, because that would make the argument incomplete and less credible.

Overall, the course was more practical than I expected and gave me a process I can reuse instead of only a list of formatting rules.

Part B — Personal Growth & Development

At the start of the course, I could usually explain what I meant, but I did not always think far enough ahead about structure or audience. My first instinct was to include information in the order I found it.

I also tended to assume that if a sentence made sense to me, it would make sense to the reader. ENG 2003 helped me slow down and plan the message before writing it.

The skill I improved most was technical report writing. In the SMR project, I went from a broad topic statement and five source notes to a report with an executive summary, background, technical explanation, comparison tables, a Canadian example, discussion, and conclusion.

Revising from peer feedback was another major improvement. Instead of treating feedback as a sign that the whole draft was wrong, I learned to keep what was working and make targeted changes.

My cover letter also improved my professional tone, while the economics reflection helped me explain an idea in a more personal and direct way.

I still need to work on proofreading and sentence control. Some earlier work contains fragments, repeated wording, or small formatting mistakes. I want to become better at catching those issues before submitting my work.

Going forward, I think the biggest thing I will take from ENG 2003 is the habit of thinking about the reader before I start writing. Whether I am writing a lab report, explaining an engineering design, documenting code, or eventually writing for an employer, I need to think about what the reader actually needs from me.
